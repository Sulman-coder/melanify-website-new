Abstract

Polyethylene terephthalate (PET) plastic is heavily utilized in global single-serve beverage packaging [1], resulting in an estimated 500 billion to 1 trillion plastic bottles consumed globally each year [2]. Because PET plastic takes up to 500 years to degrade, the vast majority of this non-biodegradable waste accumulates in landfills and marine environments [1, 2]. Concurrently, human populations are exposed to solar ultraviolet (UV) radiation, which is carcinogenic and the leading cause of skin cancers, cataracts, and cellular DNA damage [3].

Chemical sunscreens are widely used to reduce overexposure; however, active ingredients such as oxybenzone have been linked to documented human safety risks such as skin irritation, contact dermatitis, and endocrine disruption [4, 5], as well as severe degradation of marine ecosystems through coral bleaching and toxic accumulation [4, 5]. We aim to bridge these dual challenges by converting post-consumer PET plastic waste into allomelanin, a biocompatible, broad-spectrum UV-protective biopolymer.

Our Why

THAILAND · OUR MOTIVATION

WHY MELANIFY MATTERS

As young researchers living in Thailand, we witness the dual threats of extreme environmental degradation and significant health risks every day. Our country is at a critical crossroads: our bustling metropolitan centers and rivers are overrun with mismanaged plastic garbage, while our tropical position exposes millions of citizens and coastal ecosystems to constant, harsh ultraviolet radiation.

We concluded that the two distinct crises—plastic buildup and UV exposure—are inextricably linked. Our project was created not only as a laboratory experiment, but also as an urgent response to our immediate surroundings, based on localized facts in Thailand.

UNDERSTANDING THE CHALLENGE

Two connected problems.
One opportunity.

Despite a reported 88.8% municipal waste collection rate, Thailand generates an estimated 428 kilotons of mismanaged plastic waste annually due to collection gaps and unsanitary disposal facilities [7]. While rural areas produce 70.1% of the waste exposed to environmental wash-off, extreme urban density makes Bangkok a critical leakage point [7]. Within Thailand's high-priority river catchments, just 10 districts in and around Bangkok account for 51.7% of all exposed mismanaged plastic [7]. Massive volumes of this uncollected waste are dumped directly into waterways like the Chao Phraya River [7]. Once in the river system, 47.6% of this plastic inevitably flows into the ocean, resulting in an average marine discharge of 9.3 kilotons annually [7]. This figure surges to 14.3 kilotons during the rainy season, heavily contaminating vulnerable coastal ecosystems [7].

Thailand endures severe year-round solar radiation, with the national UV index averaging 11 and 12 annually and surging to extreme levels between 13 and 14 (see Figure 1) across the country during the March hot season [8, 10]. Across major provinces like Bangkok and Phuket, a mere 15 to 20 minutes of midday exposure triggers acute sunburn [10]. The World Health Organization classifies this intense ultraviolet radiation as a direct carcinogen that damages cellular DNA, inflames skin, and weakens immunity [9]. Over time, chronic exposure leads to severe health conditions, including cataracts, premature skin aging, and life-threatening skin cancers [9]. Because global health guidelines mandate sun protection at a UV index of 3 or above [9], Thailand’s extreme solar radiation levels make daily broad-spectrum sunscreen application an essential health requirement for millions of outdoor workers and residents.

So, how is this issue
being solved right now?.

Under extreme UV levels, millions of residents and tourists across Thailand require daily sun protection, and society has relied almost entirely on synthetic chemical sunscreens. However, these contemporary sunscreens come with several hidden costs that may be detrimental to both humans and the environment.

While alternative mineral sunscreens (zinc oxide and titanium dioxide) have gained popularity, they frequently leave an undesirable chalky white cast on skin, contain nano-particles with unverified long-term aquatic toxicity, and do nothing to address our global plastic crisis [11, 12].

Current Solutions

To shield human skin from UV radiation, commercial manufacturers rely heavily on synthetic UV filters like oxybenzone, octinoxate, and octocrylene [11, 12, 13, 14]. While effective at absorbing radiation, these chemical formulations create significant health and environmental hazards.

1. Human Health Side Effects

Oxybenzone penetrates the dermal barrier into the bloodstream, acting as an endocrine disruptor linked to hormonal imbalances, skin irritation, and contact dermatitis [12, 14].

2. Marine Degradation & Aquatic Toxicity

Over 14,000 tons of sunscreen wash into oceans annually, triggering coral bleaching, larval DNA damage, and toxic tissue breakdown in aquatic wildlife [11, 13, 14].

Strict Legal Enforcement

To combat reef collapse, Thailand’s Department of National Parks has banned harmful UV filters across all marine national parks, punishing violations with fines up to 100,000 THB [15].

Our Solution

To address the dual challenges of PET plastic pollution and toxic chemical sunscreens, we engineered MELANIFY, a circular synthetic biology platform that converts post-consumer plastic waste into allomelanin, a sustainable, broad-spectrum UV-protective biopolymer.

Before engineering an enzymatic pathway, we selected our target molecule based on its structural, photoprotective, and ecological properties [16, 17, 18, 19]. Unlike human skin pigments (eumelanin and pheomelanin), which require nitrogenous precursors like L-tyrosine, allomelanin is a class of nitrogen-free melanin synthesized from aromatic catechol compounds commonly produced by fungi and plants [18].

OUR APPROACH

Engineering Strategy: Dual-Host Division of Labor

Building a whole metabolic pathway from a complicated polymer (PET) to a polymerized pigment (allomelanin) within a single microbial host results in metabolic strain, severe cellular toxicity, and enzyme expression bottlenecks. MELANIFY uses a dual-host approach to address this, splitting the metabolic load between two specialized chassis:

01

Pichia pastoris

Secretory Host

Utilized for high-density extracellular enzyme secretion, breaking down solid PET plastic outside the cell without requiring cell lysis.

02

Escherichia coli

Bioconversion Host

Expresses membrane-displayed enzymes, intracellular metabolic operons, and export transporters to import PET monomers and convert them into catechol.

To address both plastic pollution and reef degradation simultaneously, MELANIFY bridges the gap with a single biological pathway.

Step 01: Starting Material: PET Plastic Waste

  • Input: Post-consumer polyethylene terephthalate (PET) bottles and microplastics.
  • Process: Plastic waste undergoes mechanical/thermal pre-treatment to increase surface area and reduce crystallinity, preparing the polymer chains for biological cleavage.
  • Role in Pathway: Transforms a persistent marine pollutant into an inexpensive, carbon-rich feedstock.

Step 02: Secreted Enzymatic Cleavage: HotPETase

  • Host Organism: Pichia pastoris (Secretion System)
  • Biological Tool: HotPETase (thermostable PETase variant)
  • Mechanism: Engineered P. pastoris secretes HotPETase directly into the culture medium. HotPETase hydrolyzes the ester bonds of insoluble PET polymer, breaking down plastic chains into soluble mono-(2-hydroxyethyl) terephthalate (MHET) monomers along with minor fractions of terephthalic acid (TPA) and ethylene glycol.

Step 03: Surface-Displayed Hydrolysis: MHETase

  • Host Organism: E. coli (Surface Display System)
  • Biological Tool: Surface-anchored MHETase
  • Mechanism: To prevent intracellular metabolic burden and transport bottlenecks, MHETase is displayed on the outer membrane of E. coli. Membrane-bound MHETase rapidly cleaves the ester linkage in MHET, yielding pure terephthalic acid (TPA) and ethylene glycol without requiring substrate uptake into the cell cytoplasm.

Step 04: Intracellular Conversion: TPA → PCA

  • Host Organism: E. coli (Bioconversion Chassis)
  • Biological Tools: tphA1, tphA2, tphA3 (TPA dioxygenase complex) and tphB (1,2-dihydroxy-3,5-cyclohexadiene-1,4-dicarboxylate dehydrogenase)
  • Mechanism: TPA enters the E. coli cytoplasm through specific transporters. The multi-component TPA dioxygenase system (tphA1A2A3) oxygenates TPA into a dihydrodiol intermediate, which is rapidly oxidized by tphB to form protocatechuic acid (PCA / 3,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid).

Step 05: Decarboxylation to Precursor: PCA → Catechol

  • Host Organism: E. coli (Bioconversion Chassis)
  • Biological Tools: AroY (Protocatechuate decarboxylase), EcdB, and EcdD
  • Mechanism: AroY catalyzes the non-oxidative decarboxylation of PCA to produce catechol. Because AroY requires a prenylated flavin mononucleotide (prFMN) cofactor for activity, the helper enzymes EcdB (prFMN synthase) and EcdD (cofactor maturation assistant) are co-expressed to ensure maximum catalytic turnover and stability of AroY.

Step 06: Polymerization & Final Yield: Catechol → Allomelanin

  • Reaction Type: Open-air oxidative biopolymerization (Cell-free / Secreted output)
  • Catalytic Conditions: Open-air exposure, slightly basic pH, and trace CuSO4 catalysts.
  • Mechanism: Catechol molecules undergo auto-oxidation and oxidative polymerization catalyzed by trace copper ions (Cu2+). The catechol monomers condense into a nitrogen-free, cross-linked biopolymer matrix—allomelanin—yielding our broad-spectrum, reef-safe UV protectant.

Ensuring GMO-Free Final Product

Because allomelanin is harvested as an isolated, cell-free biopolymer via open-air oxidative polymerization, zero engineered organisms remain in the final material. Learn more about our containment protocols on our Safety Page.

Future Implications

Contributions to the Scientific Community

Circular Plastic Upcycling

Establishes a synthetic biology pipeline that converts post-consumer PET waste into a high-value, reef-safe functional biopolymer.

Dual-Host Engineering

Demonstrates a modular division of labor—using P. pastoris for efficient enzyme secretion and E. coli for membrane-bound conversion—to mitigate cellular metabolic burden.

Enzymatic Decarboxylation Module

Validates the co-expression of the AroY decarboxylase with its maturation helpers (EcdB and EcdD) for converting pathway intermediates into catechol precursors.

Strategic Avenues for Expansion

Broadening Plastic Feedstocks

Integrating additional degradation enzymes to process alternative polymer waste streams into aromatic precursors.

Process Scale-Up

Transitioning from batch bioconversion to optimized, scalable systems for cell-free allomelanin harvesting and production.
